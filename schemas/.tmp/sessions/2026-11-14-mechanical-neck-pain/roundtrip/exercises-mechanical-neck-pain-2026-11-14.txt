Mechanical Neck Pain / Stiff Neck
Novice exercise catalogue
Report date: 14 November 2026
Search completed: 14 November 2026
Scope and safety: This catalogue is for adults with uncomplicated nonspecific/mechanical neck pain or stiffness. It is not a diagnosis. Do not use it as a substitute for assessment after major trauma or with progressive weakness, loss of coordination, severe unusual headache or neck pain, fever or systemic illness, cancer-related concerns, significant arm numbness or weakness, dizziness or visual/speech symptoms. Stop and seek advice if these occur. Choose comfortable ranges and use report-level monitoring rather than forcing symptoms.
Begin with one mobility exercise, one neck-control exercise and one shoulder or general-activity exercise. A starting dose is usually 1-2 sets, two or three days per week, with easy movement on other days. Progress only when control is good and next-day recovery is stable. The evidence category describes the broader exercise approach; it does not show that one named exercise is uniquely superior.
Mobility and Movement Confidence
1. Comfortable cervical rotation
Purpose: Practise turning the head with less guarding and maintain comfortable neck movement.
Exercise type: Mobility and motor-control
Targeted musclegroups: Cervical rotators; deep neck stabilisers; upper back postural muscles
Targeted specific muscles: musculus sternocleidomastoideus; musculus splenius capitis; musculus splenius cervicis; musculus longus colli; musculus longus capitis; musculus semispinalis capitis; musculus trapezius
Equipment and environment: Stable chair or standing space; mirror optional; no equipment required.
Starting position and set-up: Sit with both feet flat and the pelvis supported, or stand with feet hip-width apart. Let the arms rest. Look straight ahead without pulling the chin sharply backward. Keep the shoulders relaxed.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Breathe in comfortably. (2) Slowly turn the head to one side as if looking over the shoulder. (3) Pause briefly before strain or compensation begins. (4) Return to the middle and repeat to the other side.
Range and direction: Rotate left and right in a smooth, comfortable arc. Keep the trunk facing forward and do not push into a hard end range.
Breathing: Breathe normally; exhale gently during the turn and do not hold the breath.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 5-8 repetitions each side, resting 20-30 seconds between sets.
Expected sensation: Mild movement effort or a gentle stretch is acceptable. Sharp, electric, spreading or dizzy symptoms are not expected.
Regression: Use a smaller arc, perform the movement lying with the head supported, or make only two or three repetitions at a time.
Progression: Increase the range gradually, add a two-second pause near the comfortable limit, or practise during a functional task such as turning toward a conversation partner.
Common errors and corrections: Lifting a shoulder - let it drop and move the head only; twisting the chest - keep the breastbone facing forward; rushing - slow the turn and pause before strain; forcing range - return to the last comfortable point.
Evidence category: Cervical mobility within active exercise and self-management
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial for movement confidence and mobility; no clear superiority of this exact drill.
Source: El-Allawy et al. 2025; Corp et al. 2020; Blanpied et al. 2017.
2. Supine chin nod
Purpose: Build low-load control and endurance of the deep neck flexors without lifting the head.
Exercise type: Motor-control and endurance
Targeted musclegroups: Deep cervical flexors; cervical stabilisers
Targeted specific muscles: musculus longus colli; musculus longus capitis; musculus rectus capitis anterior; musculus rectus capitis lateralis; musculus sternocleidomastoideus
Equipment and environment: Exercise mat or firm bed; thin folded towel if the head needs support.
Starting position and set-up: Lie on the back with knees bent and feet supported. Place the head comfortably on the floor or a thin towel. Look toward the ceiling and let the jaw stay relaxed with teeth apart.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Breathe out slowly. (2) Make a small nod as though saying yes, lengthening the back of the neck. (3) Keep the head resting; do not press it hard into the surface. (4) Relax fully before repeating.
Range and direction: Use a very small forward nod. The head should not lift, slide or turn, and the throat should not be squeezed.
Breathing: Exhale during the nod and breathe normally during the hold.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 5-8 repetitions, holding each nod for 3-5 seconds, with 30-45 seconds rest.
Expected sensation: Gentle effort deep at the front of the neck is possible. Strong superficial neck tension, headache or jaw clenching indicates that the effort is too large.
Regression: Make a smaller nod for one second, place a little more towel under the head, or practise only finding a relaxed middle position.
Progression: Increase the hold to 8-10 seconds, add repetitions, or later practise the same controlled head position while sitting and moving the arms.
Common errors and corrections: Lifting the head - keep the skull heavy; clenching the jaw - separate the teeth and relax the tongue; pushing hard - use a smaller nod; holding the breath - count aloud softly.
Evidence category: Deep neck flexor and cervical motor-control exercise
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial as one reasonable exercise approach; not proven superior to all other progressive programmes.
Source: Blanpied et al. 2017; Jones et al. 2024; El-Allawy et al. 2025.
3. Seated thoracic extension over a chair
Purpose: Practise upper-back movement so the neck does not need to provide all extension during sitting and reaching.
Exercise type: Thoracic mobility
Targeted musclegroups: Thoracic spinal extensors; shoulder-girdle stabilisers
Targeted specific muscles: musculus erector spinae; musculus semispinalis thoracis; musculus multifidus; musculus trapezius; musculus rhomboideus major; musculus rhomboideus minor
Equipment and environment: Firm chair without wheels, with a low or moderately high back; open space behind the chair.
Starting position and set-up: Sit with feet flat and hands crossed over the chest. Position the chair back across the upper back, below the shoulder blades, not against the neck. Keep the head aligned comfortably over the trunk.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Inhale. (2) Gently lean the upper back over the chair back while the pelvis stays on the seat. (3) Move only through a comfortable upper-back range. (4) Return upright while exhaling.
Range and direction: Extend through the upper back rather than throwing the head backward. Keep the lower back and neck relaxed enough that the movement feels distributed.
Breathing: Inhale as the chest opens; exhale as you return.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 6-10 repetitions with 30 seconds rest.
Expected sensation: Gentle opening across the chest and upper back. Neck compression, sharp pain or dizziness is not expected.
Regression: Use a smaller lean, place a towel over the chair back, or perform a tall seated chest lift without leaning.
Progression: Use two chair-back levels, add a slow three-second return, or combine the movement with relaxed arm reaches.
Common errors and corrections: Throwing the head back - keep the gaze level; arching only the lower back - move the ribs over the chair back; sliding off the seat - keep the feet wide and the pelvis grounded.
Evidence category: Mobility and general exercise adjunct
Evidence strength: Uncertain for neck-specific outcomes; reasonable when it improves comfortable movement and task tolerance.
Source: El-Allawy et al. 2025; Corp et al. 2020.
Strength and Endurance
4. Cervical isometric rotation into the hand
Purpose: Practise neck muscle endurance without moving into a large rotation range.
Exercise type: Isometric strengthening and motor-control
Targeted musclegroups: Cervical rotators; deep neck stabilisers
Targeted specific muscles: musculus sternocleidomastoideus; musculus splenius capitis; musculus splenius cervicis; musculus semispinalis capitis; musculus obliquus capitis inferior; musculus longus colli
Equipment and environment: Chair or standing space; one hand provides light resistance.
Starting position and set-up: Sit tall with feet flat. Place the palm against the side of the head above the ear, with the elbow relaxed. Keep the jaw loose and shoulders down.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Turn the eyes and head only to a comfortable middle position. (2) Press the head gently into the hand as if beginning to turn, while the hand prevents movement. (3) Hold, breathe, and release gradually. (4) Repeat on the other side.
Range and direction: There should be almost no visible head movement. Use light pressure directed straight sideways, not downward or backward.
Breathing: Breathe continuously and speak a few words during the hold if needed to avoid bracing.
Beginner dose and rest: 1 set of 4-6 holds per side, 3-5 seconds each, with 20-30 seconds between sides.
Expected sensation: Mild, even neck effort. Headache, sharp pain, dizziness or arm symptoms are warning sensations.
Regression: Use only fingertip contact and 10-20 percent effort, or practise the hand position without pressing.
Progression: Increase holds to 8-10 seconds or use a small towel loop for consistent light resistance, but do not chase maximal force.
Common errors and corrections: Pushing maximally - reduce effort; shrugging - lower the shoulder; bending the trunk - sit against a backrest; breath-holding - use a quiet count.
Evidence category: Cervical strengthening and endurance
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial as a tolerable strengthening option; exact dose and superiority are uncertain.
Source: Blanpied et al. 2017; El-Allawy et al. 2025.
5. Resistance-band row
Purpose: Strengthen the upper back and practise relaxed shoulder-blade control during pulling.
Exercise type: Resistance strengthening and endurance
Targeted musclegroups: Scapular retractors; shoulder extensors; trunk postural muscles
Targeted specific muscles: musculus trapezius; musculus rhomboideus major; musculus rhomboideus minor; musculus latissimus dorsi; musculus deltoideus; musculus biceps brachii; musculus erector spinae
Equipment and environment: Light resistance band anchored at chest height to a closed, sturdy anchor; clear space; never use a loose door handle.
Starting position and set-up: Stand facing the anchor with feet hip-width apart, knees soft and spine comfortable. Hold one end in each hand with arms reaching forward. Step back until the band has light tension and keep the anchor secure.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Exhale and draw the hands toward the lower ribs. (2) Let the elbows travel back without shrugging. (3) Pause briefly with the shoulder blades gently moving toward each other. (4) Return slowly until the arms are straight without losing posture.
Range and direction: Pull back and slightly down; do not force the shoulder blades together or push the head forward. The trunk stays broadly still.
Breathing: Exhale during the pull and inhale during the return; breathe continuously.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 8-12 repetitions with 60-90 seconds rest.
Expected sensation: Work in the back of the shoulders and between the shoulder blades. Neck gripping or shoulder pain means reduce resistance or range.
Regression: Use a lighter band, stand closer to the anchor, or perform the same elbow movement without a band.
Progression: Step farther away, use a stronger band, add a one-second pause, or increase to 2-3 sets after control is consistent.
Common errors and corrections: Shrugging - keep the collarbones broad; jutting the chin - look straight ahead; leaning backward - use less band tension; snapping the band forward - return slowly.
Evidence category: Shoulder-girdle and scapular strengthening
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial as one reasonable exercise approach; scapular treatment has low to moderate and heterogeneous evidence, without clear universal superiority.
Source: Chen et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2024; Blanpied et al. 2017.
6. Wall push-up
Purpose: Build pushing strength and shoulder-blade control with low whole-body load.
Exercise type: Functional resistance strengthening
Targeted musclegroups: Chest and shoulder muscles; elbow extensors; serratus anterior; trunk stabilisers
Targeted specific muscles: musculus pectoralis major; musculus pectoralis minor; musculus triceps brachii; musculus serratus anterior; musculus deltoideus; musculus rectus abdominis; musculus obliquus externus abdominis
Equipment and environment: Clear wall with no loose objects; non-slip shoes if standing.
Starting position and set-up: Face the wall and place palms at chest height, a little wider than the shoulders. Walk the feet back about one to two feet so the body forms a straight, comfortable line from head to ankles.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Bend the elbows and bring the chest toward the wall. (2) Keep the head in line with the trunk and allow the shoulder blades to move naturally. (3) Press the wall away until the elbows are straight but not locked.
Range and direction: Move toward and away from the wall in a straight line. Use a range that keeps the neck relaxed and the shoulders below the ears.
Breathing: Inhale toward the wall and exhale as you push away.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 6-12 repetitions with 60 seconds rest.
Expected sensation: Effort in the chest, arms and front of the shoulders. Mild trunk effort is expected; neck pain should not increase progressively.
Regression: Stand closer to the wall or perform only the return-to-standing phase.
Progression: Move the feet farther away, use a sturdy counter, or progress to an incline push-up only when the wall version is controlled.
Common errors and corrections: Dropping the head - keep eyes on the wall; flaring elbows - aim them diagonally back; arching the lower back - step closer and gently brace; locking the elbows - finish softly.
Evidence category: General resistance and functional exercise
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial as a general strengthening choice; direct neck-specific evidence for this exercise is limited.
Source: Jones et al. 2024; Corp et al. 2020.
7. Band shoulder external rotation
Purpose: Strengthen the rotator-cuff and shoulder stabilisers while keeping the neck relaxed.
Exercise type: Resistance strengthening
Targeted musclegroups: Rotator cuff; scapular stabilisers; upper arm stabilisers
Targeted specific muscles: musculus infraspinatus; musculus teres minor; musculus deltoideus; musculus trapezius; musculus serratus anterior; musculus rhomboideus major
Equipment and environment: Light resistance band attached at elbow height; sturdy anchor; towel optional.
Starting position and set-up: Stand sideways to the anchor. Hold the band with the outside hand, bend that elbow to 90 degrees, and keep the elbow lightly against the side with a towel between elbow and ribs. Start with the forearm across the abdomen.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Keep the elbow near the ribs. (2) Rotate the forearm outward until it reaches a comfortable angle. (3) Pause briefly. (4) Return slowly without letting the band pull the arm across the body.
Range and direction: The upper arm stays near the trunk while the forearm moves outward. Do not twist the trunk or lift the shoulder.
Breathing: Exhale during the outward rotation and inhale on the return.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-2 sets of 8-12 repetitions per side with 60 seconds rest.
Expected sensation: Moderate effort around the back and side of the shoulder. Shoulder pain or neck gripping indicates that the band is too strong or the range too large.
Regression: Use no band and practise the movement, or use a very light band with a smaller range.
Progression: Increase band resistance or add a two-second pause after the basic version is easy and controlled.
Common errors and corrections: Elbow drifting away - use the towel; shrugging - lower the shoulder; rotating the body - face forward; fast return - slow the band back.
Evidence category: Shoulder-girdle strengthening adjunct
Evidence strength: Uncertain for neck-specific outcomes but reasonable for shoulder capacity and exercise variety.
Source: Chen et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2024.
General Activity and Self-Management
8. Comfortable walking
Purpose: Maintain general activity, confidence and circulation without requiring a specialized neck exercise.
Exercise type: Aerobic and self-management activity
Targeted musclegroups: Lower-limb muscles; trunk postural muscles; shoulder-girdle muscles
Targeted specific muscles: musculus gluteus maximus; musculus gluteus medius; musculus quadriceps femoris; musculus triceps surae; musculus erector spinae; musculus trapezius
Equipment and environment: Comfortable shoes and a safe, level route or treadmill; optional support for balance.
Starting position and set-up: Stand tall enough to see the path, with arms relaxed and gaze forward. Choose a route where you can slow down or stop easily.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Begin at an easy pace. (2) Let the arms swing naturally if comfortable. (3) Keep breathing conversationally. (4) Finish before substantial fatigue and note recovery later that day and the next morning.
Range and direction: Walk forward on a predictable surface. Do not force the neck into a fixed posture; allow natural small movements.
Breathing: Breathe normally and slow the pace if conversation becomes difficult.
Beginner dose and rest: 5-10 minutes once or twice daily, or several two-minute walks, with rest as needed.
Expected sensation: Mild whole-body effort and warmth. Increasing neck pain, dizziness or neurological symptoms are not expected.
Regression: Walk for one to three minutes indoors or use a supported stationary option.
Progression: Add one to five minutes every few sessions, increase frequency, or vary the route before increasing speed.
Common errors and corrections: Staring at the ground - look several steps ahead; bracing the shoulders - let the arms swing; starting too fast - use a conversational pace; one long session only - distribute movement across the day.
Evidence category: General activity and aerobic exercise
Evidence strength: Supported as a low-barrier active-care option; neck-specific superiority over other activity is not established.
Source: Corp et al. 2020; El-Allawy et al. 2025; Jones et al. 2024.
9. Seated movement break
Purpose: Interrupt prolonged static sitting and rehearse comfortable changes of position.
Exercise type: Mobility and activity-break routine
Targeted musclegroups: Cervical and thoracic postural muscles; hip and lower-limb muscles
Targeted specific muscles: musculus longus colli; musculus trapezius; musculus erector spinae; musculus gluteus maximus; musculus quadriceps femoris
Equipment and environment: Stable chair with enough space to stand; desk or wall optional for balance.
Starting position and set-up: Sit with feet under the knees and hands resting on the thighs. Keep the screen or visual target at a comfortable height, but do not try to hold one perfect posture.
Step-by-step movement: (1) Turn the head gently left and right once or twice. (2) Roll the shoulders comfortably backward and forward. (3) Stand up, take several steps, and sit down with control. (4) Return to work in a different comfortable position.
Range and direction: Use small to moderate comfortable movements in several directions. The purpose is variety, not maximal stretching.
Breathing: Breathe normally throughout and avoid holding the breath during standing.
Beginner dose and rest: 1-3 minutes every 30-60 minutes of prolonged sitting, adjusted to work demands.
Expected sensation: Reduced stiffness and mild whole-body movement. Symptoms should settle or remain stable after the break.
Regression: Change position while remaining seated and perform one gentle movement at a time.
Progression: Add a short walk, two sit-to-stands, or a few wall push-ups when comfortable.
Common errors and corrections: Holding a rigid posture - allow variation; forcing neck range - make movements smaller; skipping breaks until pain is high - use a timer or task transition; standing with locked knees - keep them soft.
Evidence category: Education, ergonomic exposure management and general activity
Evidence strength: Probably beneficial for comfort and participation; workstation or posture changes alone are not clearly sufficient treatment.
Source: Valenza-Pena et al. 2023; Jones et al. 2024; Corp et al. 2020.
Report-Level Monitoring
Track what was done, how controlled it felt, symptoms during the session and recovery by the following day. Reduce range, repetitions, resistance or frequency if symptoms repeatedly flare. Seek clinical assessment for new spreading pain, weakness, numbness, balance change, severe headache, dizziness, visual or speech symptoms, fever, major trauma or other warning signs. Exercise selection should be adjusted to the person's presentation, goals, preference, access, confidence and response.
